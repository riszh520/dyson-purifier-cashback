## The naming code, decoded in about ninety seconds

Dyson's letters describe what the machine does besides filtering, not how well it filters.

- **TP** — tower purifier, cooling fan only
- **HP** — hot and cool, so it doubles as a space heater
- **PH** — purifies and humidifies
- **BP** — Big+Quiet, the wide-coverage series for large rooms
- **PC** — Purifier Cool, the naming Dyson uses for retail-channel models
- **SP / HushJet** — the newer, compact, quieter format

The number is a generation or spec tier. Older number, older sensor package. That's why a TP07 and a TP09 sit on the same shelf at noticeably different prices while looking the same in photos.

## What you're actually paying for

Every current Dyson purifier uses the same core: a 360° filter stack that Dyson describes as fully sealed to HEPA H13 standard, capturing 99.95% of particles down to 0.1 microns. If you've read purifier marketing before, that "sealed" word is the part that matters. Plenty of budget purifiers use HEPA media and then leak dirty air around the filter housing edges, so a chunk of what you're paying Dyson for is the gasket, not the paper.

Above the HEPA layer sits activated carbon for gases and odours — cooking smells, pet odour, VOCs. Some models add a third element: a Cryptomelane catalytic filter that breaks formaldehyde down into water and carbon dioxide rather than trapping it. Because it destroys the gas instead of storing it, that layer doesn't saturate and doesn't need replacing, which changes the maths on running costs.

Formaldehyde matters if you've recently bought furniture, laid laminate, repainted, or moved into a renovation. Those materials off-gas for months, and most purifiers on the market don't touch it.

## Picking by situation, not by spec sheet

**A bedroom under about 200 square feet, no allergies beyond dust.** A TP07 or TP10 is the right size. You'll never use the top speeds, and the night mode drops the display and the fan down to something you can sleep next to. If you know you won't touch an app, the TP10 does the same filtration for less.

**A household with a cat or a dog.** The filter stack matters less than the auto mode and the airflow. Pet dander settles and then re-suspends when anyone walks through the room, so a purifier that ramps up on its own does more than one running at a fixed low speed. The bladeless design is also genuinely useful around animals and small children.

**New furniture, a renovation, or a newly built flat.** This is the one scenario where the extra money for the TP09, HP09 or BP04 makes sense, because of the catalytic formaldehyde layer. If your home has been settled for years, that sensor will rarely trigger and you're paying for something that sits idle.

**Dense city traffic, low floor, windows facing a main road.** NO₂ is the pollutant people forget. The De-NOx variants are built for that, and Dyson markets the PC2 line specifically at apartment dwellers near heavy traffic.

**A large open-plan living area.** The BP04 is the model to look at — the Big+Quiet series projects air much further and covers rooms several times the size of a typical Cool tower. Two TP07s in a 60 m² open plan will not do what one BP04 does.

**A cold flat in winter, or a very dry air-conditioned room.** The HP series adds heating; the PH series adds humidification. Both are worth the premium only if you'd otherwise be running a separate heater or humidifier in the same spot.

## The running cost that doesn't make the headline

Filters. Expect to replace the HEPA and carbon layers roughly once a year, and budget somewhere between $50 and $100 per swap depending on the model — PCMag's 2026 purifier guide puts Dyson replacement filters in that bracket and notes it as the main ongoing cost. Over five years that's a real number on top of the purchase price.

The formaldehyde models soften this slightly: the catalytic layer is permanent, so you're only swapping the HEPA and carbon.

Power draw is the smaller line item. These towers run somewhere in the 40–100W range depending on model and fan speed, and on auto mode they spend most of their life at the low end of that.

## Questions people ask before committing

**Which Dyson purifier is best for allergies?**
Any of the sealed HEPA H13 models will filter pollen, dust mite debris, dander and mould spores. The formaldehyde models only matter if you have a formaldehyde source — new furniture, fresh paint, recent renovation. Otherwise you're paying for a sensor that will sit quiet.

**Is a Dyson purifier worth it compared with a cheaper HEPA unit?**
Dyson doesn't publish a CADR figure, which makes direct performance comparisons awkward, and independent testing has put its CADR-per-dollar below cheaper brands. What you're buying is sealed construction, a usable app plus sensor suite, and a bladeless fan you'll actually leave running. If pure filtration per dollar is your only criterion, look elsewhere. If you want one object doing purification and air movement without being an eyesore, the maths changes.

**Does the fan cool the room?**
No. It moves air across your skin, which feels cooler, but it doesn't drop the room temperature the way an air conditioner does. The HP models add actual heating; nothing in the range adds cooling.

**How often do filters need replacing?**
Roughly annually on daily use. The app on the smart models tracks remaining filter life and notifies you. The catalytic formaldehyde layer never needs replacing.

**Can I stack a Dyson sale with cashback?**
Yes on price and bundles, no on most promo codes. Dyson's own on-site codes and codes promoted on the cashback platform are typically fine; anything else risks voiding the transaction.

**Can I buy from a reseller and still get cashback?**
Only where that reseller is itself listed as a cashback merchant. In Singapore, for example, Dyson cashback applies to purchases made directly at Dyson's own store — Sephora explicitly excludes Dyson, and most department stores and marketplace listings aren't cashback channels for Dyson at all.
